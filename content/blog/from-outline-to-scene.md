An outline can tell you that two people argue about leaving town. A scene needs to show what they do to get their way. The distance between those two things is where the writing begins.

## Start with the immediate objective

Give a character something they can pursue in this scene. “Find happiness” is a story-level desire. “Get the keys before the train leaves” is an immediate objective. The second creates actions you can put on a page.

The objective does not have to be physical. A character might want an apology, a promise, or permission to stop pretending.

## Put resistance in the room

Resistance can come from another person, a practical obstacle, or the character’s own competing need. Make it specific. A character who wants the keys but refuses to explain why gives the other person a reason to withhold them.

Write a sentence for each side of the exchange. If both people want the same outcome, find the disagreement about how to get there or what it will cost.

## Enter close to the pressure

Try starting later than your first instinct. You may not need the arrival, the greeting, and the explanation before the interesting part. Begin where the objective meets resistance and add only the context a reader needs.

This is an experiment, not a rule. A quiet opening may establish an important contrast. Keep it when it changes how the audience experiences what follows.

## End with a consequence

The character can succeed, fail, or discover that the original goal is no longer enough. What matters is that the outcome creates a new condition for the story.

Write the scene’s starting condition and ending condition beside each other. If you cannot tell them apart, look for the decision, revelation, or action that could make the scene matter.

## Draft before polishing

Write a rough version that makes the objective, resistance, and consequence visible. Then revise the dialogue and action for precision. A beautifully phrased line cannot do the work of a missing dramatic turn.

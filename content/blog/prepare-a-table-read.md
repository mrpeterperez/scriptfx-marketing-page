A table read gives you a different relationship with the page. Dialogue that felt effortless while typing may sound repetitive aloud. A pause you imagined may not be clear to anyone else. The goal is to notice those differences before deciding what to change.

## Choose a question for the read

Start with one question: does the conflict arrive soon enough? Can a listener follow the scene without an explanation? Do two characters sound too similar? A specific question turns a broad reaction into something you can use.

Read the whole scene when you can. An isolated line loses the setup and consequence that make it work.

## Prepare the page

Check character names for consistency. Make scene headings and action easy to distinguish from dialogue. Remove temporary notes that a reader might mistake for part of the scene, and decide who will read the action.

Keep a dated copy of the draft you are hearing. Your notes should point back to that version, even if you start rewriting immediately afterward.

## Decide who reads what

Assign a reader or generated voice to every speaking character. Decide whether narration includes action, scene headings, and parentheticals. Too little context can make a scene confusing; too much narration can interrupt the conversation.

For a generated read, listen to a short section first. Check the cast and pronunciation before generating a longer sequence. A performance choice can influence your reaction to a line, so distinguish a delivery problem from a writing problem.

## Take notes without rewriting everything

Use three simple columns: moment, observation, question. “The exchange slows down after the phone call” is an observation. “Could one response do the job of these three?” is a useful question. “Bad dialogue” is neither specific nor actionable.

Let the scene finish before discussing it. Notice where listeners become curious, lose track of the objective, or react differently than you expected.

## Turn the read into a revision

Choose one or two changes with the largest effect on the scene. Revise, then hear the changed section in context. Keep a line because it serves the scene, not because it sounded good in isolation.
